How to read elasticity

Think response, not just direction.

Elastic demand · |ε| > 1

Price changes move volume more.

Fashion, Beauty, Electronics, and Home are estimated as elastic. A 1% price rise corresponds to a larger percentage fall in units sold, all else equal.

Inelastic demand · |ε| < 1

Grocery reacts less to price.

Its estimate is −0.70: a 1% price increase is associated with about a 0.70% decrease in units sold in the fitted model.

Elasticity (ε) = % change in units sold ÷ % change in price.
For example, ε = −2 means a 1% price increase is associated with about a 2% drop in units.

Explore patterns

Review sales, price, promotion, and channel patterns before fitting category models.

Estimate elasticity

Fit a log–log OLS regression per category with HC3 robust standard errors and controls for competitor price, promotion, time, channel, and product.

Simulate & validate

Compare revenue and assumed-profit scenarios, apply a ±20% move limit, then demonstrate a Welch test and bootstrap interval.

07 / Responsible interpretation

What this analysis can—and can't—say

Useful for learning the workflow

  • Shows how demand sensitivity can differ across categories.
  • Separates revenue optimization from profit optimization.
  • Includes uncertainty estimates and an example experiment design.
  • Uses the same category-level recommendation for everyone; there are no customer-level or protected attributes in the model.

Not a substitute for real-world evidence

  • Synthetic data is cleaner and more controlled than real retail data.
  • Unit costs and margins are assumptions, so profit projections can shift materially.
  • The constant-elasticity model can mislead at extreme prices; inventory, advertising, and competitor reactions are not fully modeled.
  • Observational price changes can be confounded. Validate with a real, well-designed A/B test before rollout.
